# How a lawyer redlines

A redline is a negotiation document. The other side drafted the NDA; your mark-up shows them the *least* you need to change to make it acceptable for your client. Small, precise edits are easier for them to accept, look reasonable, and keep the focus on the points that matter. Replacing whole clauses with your own wording reads as "we rewrote your document", invites a fight over every word, and often loses good drafting that was already there.

**The review tells you what to achieve. The redline achieves it inside their language.** Do not paste the playbook's sample clauses over their text. Use the samples only to know the substance you need.

## The ladder: always use the lowest step that fixes the problem

1. **Strike or swap a word or short phrase.** The most common edit.
   - "within two (2) business days" → "promptly"
   - "the Disclosing Party may withhold in its sole discretion" → "not to be unreasonably withheld"
2. **Add a qualifier.** Soften an absolute without touching the rest.
   - "Recipient shall notify the Discloser" → "Recipient shall, *to the extent legally permitted and reasonably practicable,* notify the Discloser"
   - "comply with the Discloser's security policies" → "comply with the Discloser's *reasonable written* security policies *as agreed by Recipient*"
3. **Flip who the clause applies to.** Make one-sided clauses mutual by changing the party words, not by rewriting.
   - "*Recipient* may not assign…" → "*Neither party* may assign…"
   - "the name or logo of *the Company*" → "the name or logo of *the other party*"
4. **Add a carve-out or proviso** at the end of their sentence.
   - "…, *except to the extent reasonably necessary for the Purpose*"
   - "…; *provided that nothing in this Agreement restricts Recipient from using its own independently developed know-how*"
5. **Add a new sentence** to their clause when something is missing (for example, a missing exclusion or a damages exclusion). Put it where it belongs, inside or right after the related clause, not in a new section at the end.
6. **Strike the whole clause.** Use this only when the clause's *concept* is unacceptable and no tweak can fix it: a non-compete, an assignment of your client's IP, a one-way indemnity, a clause whose whole purpose the playbook resists. Always explain why in the comment. Where later sections are numbered, keep the number and write "[Intentionally omitted.]" so cross-references still work.

Rewriting an entire clause with new wording is not on the ladder. If you are tempted to do it, go clause part by clause part and apply steps 1–5 instead.

## Other habits

- **Keep their style.** Use their defined terms ("the Company", "Recipient"), their spelling (US or UK), their number format ("thirty (30) days"), and their sentence patterns.
- **Keep what is already fine.** If half a clause is acceptable, its words stay untouched, even if you would have drafted them differently.
- **Choose the position deliberately.** The default is the playbook's Preferred position. Where Preferred would mean striking a clause the other side clearly cares about, and the playbook's Fallback is acceptable, you may narrow the clause to the Fallback instead of striking it. Say so in the comment so the senior can decide.
- **Don't fix what the review didn't flag.** No style edits, no "improvements" to clauses rated Preferred, Fallback or grey. The only exceptions are consequential edits the review's final check names.
- **Comments explain, the text persuades.** The comment says which playbook point the edit serves and anything the senior needs to decide. It does not repeat the edit.
- **Read the clause after the edit, accepted.** It must read as one natural sentence in their voice, with correct grammar and punctuation.
